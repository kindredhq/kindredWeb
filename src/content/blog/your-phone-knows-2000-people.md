Scroll your contacts to the bottom. Most people find somewhere between several hundred and a few thousand entries. Now count the people you've had a real conversation with this month. For most of us it's under fifteen.

That ratio isn't a personal failing. It's arithmetic.

## The number that didn't move

In the 1990s the anthropologist Robin Dunbar noticed a correlation between primate brain size and social group size, and estimated that humans can maintain roughly 150 stable relationships. The figure is famous enough to have become a bit of a cliché, and it's argued over — but the more useful part of Dunbar's work is less quoted: the 150 isn't a flat list. It's a set of layers.

Roughly: about 5 people you're genuinely intimate with. About 15 you'd call close. About 50 friends. About 150 people whose context you can hold in your head at all. Each layer costs a different amount of attention, and the inner ones are expensive.

The layers are what matter here, because they're a claim about *scarcity*. Your innermost circle isn't small because you're antisocial. It's small because closeness requires a resource — attention over time — that doesn't scale.

And that resource has not increased since Dunbar counted. Not by one person.

## What did increase

Your address book. Every conference badge scanned, every group chat joined, every LinkedIn accept, every "let me get your number" at a wedding — all of it accumulates in the same undifferentiated list as your sister.

That's the mismatch: **storage grew a hundredfold, attention grew not at all.** And an address book has no opinion about which of those 2,000 entries you'd be devastated to lose.

The result is that your attention gets allocated by whatever is loudest rather than by whatever is closest. The colleague you'll forget in two years messages daily, so you reply daily. Your oldest friend never pushes, so months pass. Nobody chose that ranking. Your inbox chose it.

## The layers are the tool

Here's the practical turn, and it's the idea Kindred is built around: if the inner layers are the scarce thing, then the useful move is to make them explicit rather than leaving them implicit.

Most people have never actually named their inner circle. It's a feeling, not a list — and feelings don't defend themselves against a busy Tuesday. Writing it down changes the question from *"who should I catch up with?"* (unanswerable, so nothing happens) to *"has it been too long since I spoke to one of these fifteen?"* (answerable in seconds).

That reframe does three things:

**It exposes the drift you can't feel.** Nine weeks passes identically whether or not you notice it, and nothing in your phone marks the difference between nine days and nine months.

**It makes closeness a choice instead of an accident.** If your closest layer is only ever an unexamined feeling, whoever contacts you most will slowly occupy it by default.

**It removes the guilt from the other 1,985.** This is the part people don't expect. Naming fifteen isn't a demotion for everyone else — it's permission to stop feeling vaguely guilty about a list you were never going to be able to serve. You can't maintain 2,000 relationships. You were never supposed to.

## Small, on purpose

There's a version of this idea that goes wrong: treating people as a portfolio, scoring them, optimising the network. That's not it, and it produces exactly the kind of person you can tell is doing it.

The point is the opposite of maximising. It's admitting the number is small, deciding on purpose who's in it, and then actually protecting it — because the alternative isn't a bigger circle. The alternative is the same small circle, chosen for you by whoever happened to be loudest that week.

Fifteen names. You already know most of them. When did you last speak to the fifth one?
