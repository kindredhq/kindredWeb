Ask most people whether they have let important relationships go and they will say no. Not defensively — genuinely. They will tell you they are busy, that everyone is busy, and that the people who matter understand.

They are usually wrong, and the reason is interesting: **the information required to answer that question accurately is exactly the information human memory is worst at holding.**

Here is a test that gets around it. It takes about ninety seconds and you should do it now rather than later, because the version you do later will be quietly edited by the part of you that would like to score well.

## The test

Take a piece of paper. Do not open your phone — the phone is what we are testing.

**Step one.** Write down five people, outside the household you live in, who genuinely shaped how your life has gone. Not your five favourite people. Not the five you saw most recently. The five whose presence actually changed something: the one who made the introduction, the one who talked you out of the bad decision, the friend who showed up during the worst year, the mentor whose standards you still hear in your head.

Five names. Take a minute over it.

**Step two.** Beside each name, write the date of your last real conversation. Not a message. Not a birthday comment or a reaction to a story. A conversation — the kind where you both said something you had not planned to say.

**Step three.** Count how many were in the last three months.

## What the answer means

Most people, doing this honestly, get one or two. Sometimes zero.

But the number is not the finding. The finding is what happened in step two: **you could not answer it.** You wrote "spring?" or "sometime last year" or you sat there mentally reconstructing when you were last in that city. For at least one name — probably the one you would say matters most — you genuinely did not know.

That is the whole thing. Not that you have neglected people, but that you had no idea whether you had. There was no signal to tell you. Nothing anywhere in your life keeps that number, so it drifted for however long it drifted, and would have kept drifting.

Compare that to any other thing you care about. You know your revenue this quarter. You know roughly what you weigh, when you last trained, how much you have read this year, when the car was serviced. All of it, tracked somewhere, retrievable in seconds.

The five people who shaped your life: no idea.

## Why it fails silently

Two things make this specific problem invisible.

**The first is that nothing generates an alert.** A relationship falling apart is loud — there is a fight, a distance you can feel, something you respond to. A relationship going quiet produces nothing at all. No notification fires when it has been seven months. Silence does not ring.

Meanwhile everything else in your life is engineered to be maximally loud. The inbox escalates. The calendar interrupts. Work has deadlines that shout. Attention flows exactly where you would expect: toward whatever makes noise, and away from people who would never dream of pressuring you.

Which produces the cruel bit. **The people who ask least of you are the ones you are most likely to lose** — not because they matter less, but because their patience never registers as urgency, and urgency is how your attention gets allocated.

**The second is that you are already at capacity.** In the 1990s the anthropologist Robin Dunbar found that human social groups cluster around 150 stable relationships, with tighter layers inside — around 50 good friends, 15 close ones, 5 you would call at 2am. Those layers are not a personality trait. They are a memory constraint. Past a certain number, you cannot hold who you spoke to and when, so the tracking silently stops and you do not notice the moment it stopped.

Everyone past that boundary is maintained by chance: by who happens to email you, who lives nearby, who is good at chasing. Not by who you would choose.

## What it is not

It is not a character flaw, and this is worth saying because the usual response to the test is a wave of guilt that then goes nowhere.

Nobody in this position stopped caring. If you had stopped caring, the names would have been hard to write, and they were not — they came quickly, along with a specific memory attached to each. The caring is entirely intact. What is missing is a mechanism, and no amount of feeling worse about it supplies one.

That distinction matters practically, because guilt and systems solve different problems. Guilt makes you resolve to be better, which lasts around four days. A system just tells you when it has been eleven weeks.

## What actually changes it

Very little, honestly. The fix is unglamorous.

**Write the list down.** Not in your head — the head is what just failed the test. Fifteen names, maybe twenty. The act of committing to a list is most of the work, because it converts an unanswerable question (*who should I catch up with?*) into an answerable one (*has it been too long since one of these fifteen?*).

**Decide a rough rhythm per person.** Weekly for a handful. Monthly for the core. Twice a year for people you still want in your life but do not need to speak to often. This feels clinical for about a week and then feels obvious — you already do exactly this with birthdays and nobody considers a calendar reminder cold.

**Make something other than you keep the count.** This is the part that does not work otherwise. Any system that depends on you remembering to check it has the same failure mode as the memory it replaced.

**Then act while it is cheap.** At week two, reaching out costs six words: *"Saw this and thought of you."* At month six the same message needs a paragraph of explanation. At year two it needs an apology, which is why it never gets sent. The entire opportunity is in the gap between noticing and it becoming awkward — and that gap is only open early.

---

Go back to your five names. Pick the one where you could not date the last conversation.

You do not need a system to handle that one. You need about eleven seconds and the willingness to send six words with no preamble. Do that first, then decide whether the other fourteen are worth building something for.
