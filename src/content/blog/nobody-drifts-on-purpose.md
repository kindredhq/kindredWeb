Think of someone you haven't spoken to in over a year. Not someone you fell out with — those are different, and at least they're honest. Think of someone you simply stopped talking to.

You probably can't point to the moment. There wasn't one. There was no fight, no decision, no line in the sand. Just a message you meant to send in March that you sent in your head instead.

That's the thing worth noticing: **the relationships you lose are almost never the ones you chose to lose.** They're taken by a process so slow and so ordinary that it never once asks for your consent.

## The mechanism

Drift works in three stages, and only the first one is comfortable.

**Weeks one to three: the thought.** Something reminds you of them — a song, a street, a photo. You feel warm about it. You think, *I should call them.* The intention is genuine. You mean it entirely. And then the week does what weeks do.

At this stage the cost of reaching out is zero. You'd send four words. They'd send four back. Nothing about it requires courage.

**Month three to eight: the weight.** Now the gap has a size, and the size has become part of the message. You're no longer sending four words. You're sending four words plus an explanation for the silence, and the explanation is the hard part, because there isn't one. "Sorry, I've been meaning to message for ages" is an admission you'd rather not make in writing.

So the message gets heavier every week you don't send it — and, cruelly, the heavier it gets, the less likely you are to send it. The delay compounds itself. This is the stage where nearly everything is lost.

**Year two and beyond: the story.** Eventually the silence stops feeling like an oversight and starts feeling like a fact. You don't think *I should call them* anymore. You think *we lost touch* — a phrase that quietly turns an accident into a description of the world. Passive voice, no agent, nobody's fault.

Nothing happened. That's the whole point. Nothing is what happened.

## Why you can't feel it happening

The reason drift wins so often is that it produces no signal.

A relationship falling apart is loud — you notice, you respond, you either fix it or grieve it. A relationship going quiet produces nothing at all. There's no notification for absence. No app tells you it's been seven months since you spoke to your closest friend from university. The silence doesn't ring.

Meanwhile, everything else in your life is engineered to be *maximally* loud. Your inbox escalates. Your calendar interrupts. Work has deadlines that shout. So attention flows exactly where you'd expect it to flow: toward the things making noise, and away from the people who would never dream of pressuring you.

Here's the uncomfortable part of that. **The people who ask least of you are the ones you're most likely to lose.** Not because they matter less — often it's the reverse. Because their patience never registers as urgency, and your attention is allocated by urgency.

## What actually helps

Not willpower. If good intentions were sufficient, nobody would ever drift, because nobody intends to.

What helps is much smaller than that: a system that notices the gap and says something before the gap becomes an explanation. That's it. Not a productivity framework for your friendships. Not turning people into a pipeline. Just something outside your own memory that keeps count, so the noticing doesn't depend on a stray thought landing on a day you happen to have five free minutes.

Because the fix, at week three, is genuinely trivial. It's a message that takes eleven seconds. The entire problem is that nothing in your life reminds you to send it while it's still eleven seconds long.

That's the whole reason Kindred exists. Not to manage your relationships — you're perfectly capable of that — but to make sure the moment to act arrives while acting is still easy.
