You've drafted it a few times. Deleted it a few times. The problem isn't that you don't know what to say to them — it's that you think you owe the silence an explanation before you're allowed to say anything at all.

You don't. Here's the reframe, then the actual sentences.

## The mistake: treating the gap as the subject

Most stuck messages start by addressing the silence. *"Hey! I know it's been forever, I'm so sorry I've been terrible at keeping in touch, life has been mad…"*

Three problems with that. It opens on an apology, which asks them to reassure you — so your reconnection starts by giving them a small job. It makes the gap the topic, when the gap is the least interesting thing about the two of you. And it sets a bar: now they feel they should explain their silence too, and if they can't be bothered, the easiest reply is no reply.

The gap is not the subject. **They are the subject.** You can acknowledge time has passed in four words, or skip it entirely, and no reasonable person will hold it against you. In practice the other person is usually relieved — a good number of them were drafting the same message to you.

## Six openings that work

**1. The specific memory.** *"I walked past that terrible pizza place near your old flat today and thought of you."*

Specificity is proof. It says: you were in my head unprompted, and I remember our actual life together, not just your name. Nearly unanswerable in a good way.

**2. The useful thing.** *"Saw this and immediately thought of you."* Plus a link, a photo, a job posting, a book.

Gives them something rather than asking for something. The lowest-pressure opening there is — it can be answered with a single emoji, and that single emoji reopens the channel.

**3. The straight question.** *"How did the move go?"* / *"Did you end up taking that job?"*

Requires you to remember one real detail of their life, which is the point. It picks the thread back up exactly where you dropped it, and implies the intervening silence didn't erase what you knew about them.

**4. The honest four words.** *"Far too long. Hi."*

Sometimes the cleanest move is to name it and move on immediately. It works because it refuses to make a meal of the gap — no essay, no apology spiral. Confidence is doing the small version of the thing.

**5. The occasion.** A birthday, a new year, a piece of news about their industry, their team winning.

Occasions are permission structures. They give the message an obvious reason to exist, which does the work your explanation was trying to do.

**6. The direct ask.** *"I miss you. Coffee this month?"*

The riskiest and the strongest. For people who genuinely matter, skip the ramp entirely. Sincerity is disarming precisely because so few people use it.

## Two rules

**Don't audit the friendship.** *"I feel like we've grown apart"* and *"You never message me either"* are conversations for people who are already talking. Opening with the state of the relationship, rather than the relationship itself, converts a warm message into a difficult one.

**Send it at a human hour.** Not 2am, when it reads like a crisis. A weekday evening or a Sunday morning, when a reply is a pleasure rather than a task.

## The part that isn't about wording

None of these openings are hard. You could have written any of them yourself — you probably drafted two of them already.

Which tells you the difficulty was never linguistic. The message doesn't get sent because it never quite becomes today's problem. It stays a background intention, and background intentions lose to whatever is in the foreground, every single time, for years.

So the practical fix isn't a better script. It's making sure the thought arrives on a day you can act on it — while it's still four words rather than four paragraphs.

Pick one from the list. Send it now, before you close this tab. It's already a good message.
